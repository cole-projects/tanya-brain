# Session 13 — Client

*Date: 2026-08-28*

---

**Client:** I’d like to do more practice

**Tanya:** That's great that you want to build more practice into your life! What's one area where you feel like consistent practice could really change things for you?

**Client:** No, I want to continue practicing honing my coaching like we did in our last session

**Tanya:** Got it. So we're picking up right where we left off.

Last time you got clear on the difference between reflecting the situation back versus naming the human cost underneath it. That's the gap you were working to close.

Want to jump straight into a scenario, or is there something specific you want to focus on this round?

**Client:** Let’s use this scenario since it’s one. I just received via text from a client. The client said. We got the diagnoses back about my mother. She is in bad shape. I don't see her recovering.

**Tanya:** Okay, take a shot at it. What's your response?

**Client:** This is a diagnosis with no recovery. What does it feel like receiving that news?

**Tanya:** You got the situation right. What's missing is the weight.

"A diagnosis with no recovery" is accurate but it's clinical. It names the fact, not what it costs her. She didn't just receive medical information, she received the end of something.

Try again. Before you ask the question, land one line that names what she's actually carrying.

**Client:** This is a diagnosis without her ever coming back to who she is as your mom

**Tanya:** Yes. That's it.

You moved from the medical fact to the human loss. "Who she is as your mom" is what makes it land, because that's what's actually gone.

Now add your question. Where do you go from there?

**Client:** But you’ve been telling me to stay within their words, and those were my added words

**Tanya:** You're right to flag that. And there's a distinction worth holding.

Staying in their language means don't interpret their emotion or tell them what they feel. But naming the weight of what they said, that's not interpretation. That's compression. You took what was implicit in "I don't see her recovering" and made it explicit. That's the move.

She didn't have to say "my mom as I know her is gone" for it to be true in what she wrote. You heard it and named it. That's exactly what good coaching does.

Now go ahead and add the question.

**Client:** What does being with this diagnosis feel like

**Tanya:** Good instinct going to the felt sense. One small thing: "being with this diagnosis" still has a clinical word in it.

Try it again using her language instead.

**Client:** What are you feeling knowing her time is limited

**Tanya:** Closer. You're warmer now.

One thing to notice: "knowing her time is limited" is actually softer than what she said. She said "I don't see her recovering." That's not limited time, that's gone. She's already there.

Try once more. Stay inside the weight of what she actually wrote.

**Client:** What happens within you when you feel the weight of her absence

**Tanya:** That's the one.

"What happens within you" opens the body, not just the mind. "The weight of her absence" uses the reality she already named without softening it. And you're not asking her to explain it, you're asking her to feel into it.

That's the full move: land the cost, then open the body. You just did both.

Want to try another scenario, or go deeper on something from this one?

**Client:** Can you tell me what you would have responded?

**Tanya:** I would have said something close to what you landed on.

Maybe: "She's not coming back as the mom you've known. What happens in your body when that lands?"

The first sentence does the same thing yours did, names the loss without softening it. The second opens the body. You were right there. The gap between what I would say and what you said is smaller than you think.

What did you notice doing this one compared to last session?

**Client:** My only hesitation on this is that I think he may be making a leap of it being unrecoverable based off what the diagnosis was, but I am not sure if they were given a prognosis

**Tanya:** That's a really good clinical instinct, and it's worth separating from the coaching move.

In coaching, you work with what the client brought, not what the facts might be. She said "I don't see her recovering." That's her reality right now. Your job isn't to verify the prognosis, it's to meet her where she is. If you pause to check the facts, you've stepped out of her experience and into problem-solving.

The medical reality is hers to sort out with doctors. What she brought to you is the weight of what she's feeling right now. That's your territory.

Does that land, or does it still feel like a leap to you?

**Client:** But what if this is her making a premature declaration of some defeat when in reality there might be a possibility of healing

**Tanya:** That concern is real. And it tells me something about where your instinct goes, toward hope, toward possibility, toward not letting someone collapse prematurely.

Here's the thing though. If you redirect her toward possibility right now, what are you actually doing?

**Client:** Yes, I’m probably not meeting them where they’re at and it might make her frustrated to not be able to feel what she feels or be able to see something different if she has to ignore what she feels

**Tanya:** Exactly. You just named it precisely.

If she's carrying "I don't see her recovering" and you meet her with possibility, she has to drop what she's holding to follow you there. And she's not ready to drop it. She came to you with the weight, not with a question about odds.

There's also something else underneath your instinct worth knowing. The urge to redirect toward hope often comes from our own discomfort with helplessness. When someone is in a place we can't fix, offering possibility feels like doing something. But the client experiences it as not being heard.

The paradox is that meeting her fully in the loss is what creates the conditions for her to find her own way to hope, if and when she's ready. You can't rush her there.

What comes up for you sitting with that?

**Client:** I think you are correct and thanks for helping me sort that out

**Tanya:** You sorted it out. I just stayed with you while you did.

That instinct you caught in yourself, the pull toward hope when someone is in helplessness, that's going to come up with clients. A lot. Now you've named it in yourself, which means you'll recognize it faster next time.

Want to try another scenario or is this a good stopping point?

**Client:** The response from my client to my text with reflection in question is below. My client didn’t answer the question. 

I know both my parents are reaching the end. My Dad is 94. My mom is 88. This March they will have been married for 70 years. 
My mother's breast cancer has moved to her lungs. She also has dementia, heart disease, diabetes, and other issues. It is too much.

**Tanya:** She didn't avoid the question. She answered it by giving you more.

"It is too much" is the weight landing. She's not ready to go inward yet. She's still setting down what she's been carrying.

What do you do with this response?

**Client:** I’m not sure

**Tanya:** Stay with what she just gave you.

She's 70 years of marriage, a father at 94, a mother whose body is failing in multiple directions at once, and "it is too much." That's not one loss. That's an entire world starting to come apart.

What's the heaviest thing in what she wrote?

**Client:** That she’s feeling the weight of both parents soon not here

**Tanya:** Yes. And there's something even more specific inside that.

Seventy years of marriage. Her parents have never existed in the world without each other. She's not just losing two people. She may be watching her father lose the only person he's ever known as home.

What do you do with that?

**Client:** Ah yes. I see. I reflect that back?

**Tanya:** Try it. Write what you'd send her.

**Client:** 70 years of marriage is a long time existing together. You’re now having to watch your dad lose his life companion while you sit with the realization that you’re close to losing them both.

**Tanya:** That's good. You stayed in her world and you named two losses at once, hers and her father's.

One thing to notice. "You're now having to watch" and "you sit with the realization" are both a little managed. A little narrated from the outside.

What if you went one degree closer to the bone?

**Client:** Show me how

**Tanya:** Something like: "Your dad is about to lose the only person he's ever called home. And you're watching all of it."

Short. No narration. Just the weight, placed in front of her.

Now you try. Don't explain it, just land it.

**Client:** Your dad is losing his life partner and you’re losing your two first partners in life.

**Tanya:** That's closer. "Two first partners in life" is a real phrase, it's specific to her.

Notice what happened though. You named her loss and his in the same sentence, which softened the landing of each. The move that hits harder is one loss at a time.

Try splitting them. Land his first. Then hers. Two sentences.

**Client:** Your dad is feeling the weight of the loss of 70 years with his life partner. You are losing your first two life connections.

**Tanya:** You're getting there. The second sentence is the strongest thing you've written, "your first two life connections" is precise and real.

The first sentence still has some distance in it. "Feeling the weight of the loss" is you describing his experience from the outside. 

What if you just named what's true for him, the way she would feel it watching him?

**Client:** Youre watching your dad lose his love of his life and you’re feeling them both slip away

**Tanya:** That's it. That landed.

"Watching your dad lose the love of his life" puts her in the scene. "Feeling them both slip away" is the cost, in her body, not narrated from outside it.

You just wrote a line that could only be said to this person about this moment. That's the move.

**Client:** Do I ask a question?

**Tanya:** What does your gut say?

**Client:** My cat says to make a statement that lets him know I relate to that or at least honor the pain of that

**Tanya:** Your gut is right. She doesn't need a question yet. She needs to feel that what she just carried into the room actually landed somewhere.

So what's the statement? Write it.

**Client:** My heart hurts for you in a moment like this that is so raw and real. You are sitting in the middle of your own truth with great courage.

**Tanya:** The first sentence is real. "Raw and real" lands.

The second sentence loses it. "Sitting in the middle of your own truth with great courage" is coaching language. It pulls you out of her world and into a framework.

Stay in the feeling. What's actually true for her right now, in plain words?

**Client:** You are in one of life’s hardest moments right here

**Tanya:** Yes. That's it. Simple, direct, no framework.

"My heart hurts for you. You are in one of life's hardest moments right here."

Two sentences. Nothing extra. That's the whole move.

**Client:** Do I offer an encouraging statement of how brave he’s being in it or how he’s meeting it authentically

**Tanya:** What would she need to hear more right now, that she's brave, or that she's not alone in it?

**Client:** Not alone

**Tanya:** Then go there. Write that line.

**Client:** I’m here

**Tanya:** Perfect. That's the whole thing.
