# Session 21 — Client

*Date: 2026-09-19*

---

**Client:** More practice

**Tanya:** Same format as last time , I'll give you a scenario, you give me a reflection and a question, and then I'll give you my feedback and my version. Ready when you are.

**Client:** Ready

**Tanya:** A client says:

"I've been trying to get promoted for two years. My manager keeps telling me I'm almost there, but then the goalposts move. I'm starting to wonder if I'm just not cut out for this."

**Client:** With two years of effort and a moving target you feel like this may never happen for you. What makes you feel that you’re not cut out for it?

**Tanya:** Good instincts on both moves. Let me break it down.

The reflection landed the time and the frustration. "Moving target" was a smart grab from their language. Where it stopped short: "may never happen for you" is still situation-level. The heaviest thing in what they said isn't the promotion, it's "I'm not cut out for this." That's an identity verdict, not a situational read. The reflection has the most power when it names that weight directly.

The question had the right instinct, going to the belief underneath. The refinement: "what makes you feel" softens it slightly. "What makes you think you're not cut out for it?" or even more precisely, "What would it mean about you if that were true?" goes straight to the wound rather than asking them to explain the feeling.

Here's my version:

"Two years of showing up, and every time you get close, the line moves. And now you're starting to wonder if the problem is you. What would it mean about you if that were true?"

The reflection names the cost, not just the situation. The question goes to identity, not explanation. See the difference?

**Client:** Yes let’s do another

**Tanya:** A client says:

"My sister and I got into a huge fight last week. She said some really hurtful things. I know I should forgive her but I honestly don't want to. I feel guilty about that."

**Client:** Your sister said some things that hurt and now you’re wondering if being mad and not wanting to forgive her means something bad about you. What’s going on inside you being caught between knowing you should forgive her but not wanting to?

**Tanya:** Really strong this time. Let me show you what landed.

The reflection did something the last one didn't, it named the bind. "Caught between knowing you should and not wanting to" is exactly where she is, and you found it without interpreting. You also stayed in her experience rather than describing the situation. That's the move.

One small refinement: "means something bad about you" is close but slightly generic. The guilt she named is specifically about not wanting to forgive, so you could sharpen it to something like "not wanting to forgive makes you the wrong kind of person." That's the exact verdict underneath the guilt.

The question is good. It opens the feeling rather than asking her to explain the logic. The only thing to watch: it's a little long. Questions land harder when they're shorter. "What's it like to be caught in that?" or even just "What's the guilt actually saying about you?" cuts straight to it.

Here's my version:

"She hurt you, and now you're hurting yourself for not being ready to let it go. What is the guilt telling you it means about you that you don't want to forgive her?"

The reflection names the double bind. The question goes straight to the belief underneath the guilt. How does that land?

**Client:** Good. Ok. Try another

**Tanya:** A client says:

"I started my own business six months ago and it's not taking off the way I expected. My family keeps asking when I'm going to get a real job. And honestly, I'm starting to believe them."

**Client:** 6 months of courage to start your own business and their doubt is making you doubt that this is a real job. What does their criticism mean about tou?

**Tanya:** The instinct is right, you went for the identity layer and that's exactly where this lives. A couple of things to refine.

"6 months of courage" is interpretation. She didn't say it felt like courage. She said it's not taking off and her family is doubting her. Naming courage before she's claimed it risks her feeling like you're cheerleading rather than hearing her. Stay in what she gave you.

"Their doubt is making you doubt that this is a real job" shifted the frame slightly. The heavier thing isn't whether it's a real job, it's the last line: "I'm starting to believe them." That's the wound. She's not just hearing their doubt, she's absorbing it as truth about herself. That's where the reflection has the most power.

Also, small thing: "tou" instead of "you", in a live session that kind of thing can break the moment, so worth slowing down slightly before sending.

Here's my version:

"Six months in, the results aren't there yet, and now their voice is starting to sound like your own. What do you believe about yourself when you hear them say that?"

The reflection names what's actually happening, she's internalizing their verdict. The question goes to the belief she's forming about herself, not about the business. See how that shifts it?
