# Session 20 — Client

*Date: 2026-09-17*

---

**Client:** OK, give me a scenario and then I can do the reflection and question

**Tanya:** Here's a scenario for you:

"I've been covering for my colleague for months , she's going through something hard and I don't want to make it worse. But I'm drowning, and I haven't told anyone. I just keep showing up."

Reflect back what you're hearing, then follow it with a question.

**Client:** It sounds like Covering for your friend is now left you drowning and stopping would feel like it makes it worse. What is showing up feel like?

**Tanya:** Good instinct going to felt sense with the question.

The reflection landed the situation accurately. The edge: "drowning" is the heaviest word she gave you, and it got restated as part of the summary rather than placed at the center. The compression move would be naming what drowning is costing her, not describing the bind she's in.

Something closer to: "You've been holding this alone, and it's taking everything you have."

That's the human cost underneath the situation. The question was solid, good direction. What do you want to try next, another scenario or another pass at this one?

**Client:** What would’ve been your reflection and question?

**Tanya:** Reflection: "You've been carrying this completely alone, and it's costing you more than you're letting on."

Question: "What does it feel like to keep showing up when you're this empty?"

The reflection names the cost, not the situation. The question follows the word she actually used, drowning, without repeating it literally. It goes straight to the felt experience of the thing she's already doing rather than asking whether she'll stop.

Want another scenario?

**Client:** Yes, another scenario

**Tanya:** Here's your next one:

"My dad was just diagnosed with early-stage dementia. We're not close, we never have been. But now everyone expects me to step up and take care of him, and I don't know how to feel about that."

Reflect and question.

**Client:** You’re being asked to care on a level of closeness that hasn’t been there for a while. I’m not even sure what question to ask.

**Tanya:** The sensing was right, you felt the complexity. The reflection stayed at the situation level though, describing the dynamic rather than naming what it costs her.

The heaviest thing she said: "I don't know how to feel about that." That's not confusion, that's someone being asked to give something they were never given. The cost is in that gap.

Something closer to: "You're being asked to show up for someone who was never really there for you, and you don't even know if you're allowed to feel conflicted about that."

For the question, follow where she stopped. She trailed off into "I don't know how to feel." That's the door.

"What feeling keeps trying to come up when you sit with this?"

When you're not sure what question to ask, go to where they stopped talking. That's almost always where the weight is. Want to try another?

**Client:** Yes

**Tanya:** Here's your next one:

"I finally got the promotion I've been working toward for three years. My team threw me a little celebration, my boss said all the right things. I got home that night and just sat in my car in the driveway. I couldn't go inside."

Reflect and question.

**Client:** Tell me what reflection and question you would say

**Tanya:** You didn't try it first this time. What stopped you?

**Client:** I just feel like I have no idea where to go with this. I keep wanting to just describe what they said. I still don’t understand how to see the cost.

**Tanya:** That makes sense, and it's actually a precise diagnosis of exactly where you are.

Here's the thing: the cost is almost never stated. It lives in the gap between what happened and how they responded to it. She got everything she worked three years for, and she couldn't go inside. That gap is the cost. Something about getting the thing she wanted made her feel more alone, not less. That's what the car is about.

So the reflection names that gap: "You got exactly what you worked for, and something about it left you more alone than you expected."

The question follows the image she gave you, sitting in the car, unable to move: "What was happening in you while you were sitting there?"

The cost isn't in the words they use. It's in what they did instead of what you'd expect. She should have gone inside celebrating. She sat in her car. That's where you look.

Want to try the next scenario with that in mind?

**Client:** Yes, but how do you know that’s the gap that she was feeling alone and that that’s what the car was about. Maybe she was just overwhelmed at how exciting it was

**Tanya:** You're right, and that's an important catch.

I made an interpretive leap. "More alone than expected" is my read, not hers. She didn't say lonely. She said she couldn't go inside, and that's all we actually have.

So the honest reflection is closer to: "You'd worked three years for this moment, and when it finally came, something stopped you."

That names the gap without filling it in. The question then opens it: "What was happening in you while you were sitting there?" That question works either way, whether it's loneliness or overwhelm or something else entirely. Her answer tells you. You don't decide what the car means, you ask.

The cost becomes visible after she responds, not before. Your job in the reflection is to name that something significant is happening, without naming what it is. The question is what finds out.

Good instinct pushing back on that. Want to try the next one?

**Client:** Yes, let’s try the next

**Tanya:** "My sister and I used to talk every day. Then I set a boundary with her about how she speaks to me, and she went silent. That was eight months ago. My family says I should just apologize to keep the peace, but I can't bring myself to do it."

Reflect and question.

**Client:** If you speak up, you get the silent treatment, but if you stay silent, other people‘s voices are louder than yours and you’re feeling like you lose either way

**Tanya:** Really close, and the "lose either way" is the strongest part, that's the bind she's in and you named it.

The edge: the reflection described the dynamic from the outside rather than landing on what it costs her. What does losing either way actually take from her every day? Eight months of silence from someone she talked to daily. That's the cost living in the background of everything.

Something closer to: "You asked to be treated with basic respect, and it cost you your sister. Eight months later you're still carrying that."

That's specific to her situation in a way "lose either way" isn't quite. The bind is real, but the weight is the loss of that particular relationship, someone she talked to every single day.

Your question, what would you have followed with?

**Client:** What would it mean about you to apologize?

**Tanya:** That's a strong question. It goes straight to identity, not the situation. What would apologizing prove about her, about whether the boundary was worth it, about who she is. That's exactly the right layer.

The only refinement: it lands slightly abstract. You could make it more specific to what she actually said: "What would it say about you if you apologized for asking to be treated well?"

That uses her language and makes the identity cost more concrete. But the instinct was right, you went to the right place.

Want another scenario?

**Client:** Yes

**Tanya:** "I've been sober for two years. Nobody in my life knows except my sponsor. I go to every birthday party, every work happy hour, every dinner where everyone's drinking. I just say I'm on antibiotics. I've been lying to everyone I love for two years."

Reflect and question.

**Client:** Two years of having to lie about something you’re actually proud of. What does it feel like to hold this alone?

**Tanya:** That's your best one yet.

You found the cost without interpreting it. Two years of hiding something that deserves to be celebrated, that's the gap, and you named it cleanly. "Something you're actually proud of" is the exact tension she's carrying without you putting words in her mouth about what she feels.

The question is specific, grounded in what she gave you, and opens the emotional layer without leading her anywhere. It could land a dozen different ways and all of them are useful.

Nothing to refine. That one landed.

Want to keep going?

**Client:** Sure one more

**Tanya:** "My dad was diagnosed with early-stage dementia six months ago. I've been handling everything. Doctor appointments, finances, medication. My brother lives twenty minutes away and hasn't shown up once. Every time I bring it up he says he just can't handle it. I don't even know if I'm angry at him or just exhausted."

Reflect and question.

**Client:** It’s been six months of handling everything while knowing your brother could relieve some of your exhaustion, but he hasn’t. What happens within you when you’ve taken on this much?

**Tanya:** Good instinct on the question, and "what happens within you" is better than "how do you feel", it opens the body, not just the emotion.

The reflection is where there's still room. You described the situation accurately but landed just short of the cost. What does it actually take from her to carry this alone while her brother is twenty minutes away and choosing not to show up? The exhaustion is named, but the weight underneath it is closer to: she's grieving her father and managing everything, with no one beside her in it.

Something like: "You're losing your dad and carrying all of it, and the one person who could stand next to you in this has decided he can't."

That's the human cost. Not just exhausted, but alone in something that was never supposed to be carried alone.

Your question still works after a reflection that specific. Want to try one more, or do you want to take a pass at rewriting that reflection first?
